France, March 2026

I was slightly excited for the castles. I would have been thrilled to the extreme if I was still a child. When I was very young, every time I heard the word castle, I imagined what could be inside. I was much older when I traveled to Loire and saw enough parts of the world to not expect another shock. Despite that, when our car traveled through the open fields in the North of France and the massive castle of Chambord loomed in front, I was surprised. In fact, I was surprised enough to gasp.

The castle was of a giant scale. Towers after towers circled a rectangular region in the middle. Looking at the parade of towers as our car drove towards the parking lot, I started to imagine the things inside the way I did before. The interiors of palaces we saw in Paris were glamorous but not breathtaking. With such an exterior, this castle should be.

Finally, we bought the tickets and walked on the path that led to the castle. On the way, I continued staring at the huge structure. Many towers had balconies and I could see tourists on some. I could not wait to stand on one of those myself.

We entered the castle and everything faded into literal emptiness.

The tall doorway opened to an empty interior. There were stone arches on the ceiling that held up the roof, but besides that, nothing else seemed interesting. I looked around and searched for hidden paths that revealed gilded rooms and shining jewels and found nothing. There were only rooms made of stone with some old paintings. I saw taxidermied animals—a boar, a deer, and some smaller animals—that the kings used to hunt, but no luxurious furniture that kings used.

The only thing that took away from the disappointment was the staircase that led to the second floor. It was supposedly designed by Da Vinci and consisted of two separate staircases that brought people to the second floor without intersecting. My dad and I tried to enter from two separate sides and truly did not see each other until we reached the second floor.

The apartments of the second floor were decent, but nowhere near Versailles. The room of King Francis I, who ruled in the 16th century and constructed the castle, was perhaps the most disappointing. There was nothing other than a few pieces of furniture, and it was very cold.

We climbed up to the balcony and looked at the garden, which was less disappointing than the rooms. I realized that looking down from a great height was one of the things I loved most about the castle. I could see far away, and I have always loved heights. Exploring the castle like a maze was also enjoyable. Some rooms were hidden in obscure towers that could only be reached through specific paths. These aspects took away much of the disappointment from the empty rooms.

We later found out that the castle of Chambord was raided during the French Revolution and stripped of its interiors. I suppose I was simply too late.

## Versailles

The Palace of Versailles is something I had heard about endlessly. I had imagined something almost unreal—perhaps even furniture made of pure gold.

To spoil it: I did not find any.

The entrance lived up to expectations. There was a gilded gate that led to gilded buildings, and a statue of Louis XIV on horseback stood proudly in front. It was difficult to take a photo capturing my tall father, my small mother, and the palace all at once. Crowds of tourists filled the plaza, and lines stretched endlessly with different time slots marked out.

We were early, so we walked toward the garden.

The garden was far less crowded, and we could see everything clearly. The trees were cut into perfect geometric shapes, maintained with precision. Plants formed patterns across the ground. From above, it would resemble a vast quilt. As we walked, we realized how enormous it was—it stretched endlessly into the horizon.

Worried about missing our entry time, we sat near a fountain and simply took in the scenery. To explore the entire garden would have taken hours.

When it was finally time, we entered the palace.

Inside, the ceilings were extremely high, and the rooms were elegant. The walls were gilded, but there was still no sign of the pure gold furniture I had imagined. Crystal chandeliers hung from the ceilings, beautiful but smaller than expected. The paintings above were detailed but not particularly unique.

Then we entered the Hall of Mirrors.

That was different.

The hall was filled with light from an overwhelming number of windows. Statues seemed to glow under the sunlight, and red marble busts appeared regularly in niches along the walls. The entire space felt alive. This moment stayed with me.

Before leaving, we rented a golf cart and drove through parts of the garden. The wind brushed against my face as the scenery passed by. The scale of the garden was undeniable. Following the map, we visited fountains and statues that seemed almost alive.

By the time we finished, the palace was closing. Somehow, I realized I had enjoyed the visit—even without golden furniture.

## Southern France

During the trip, I realized it would be impossible not to mention the views outside our car windows.

Driving through the South of France was stunning. It was still winter and freezing in New Jersey, but here, the sky was clear with only faint traces of clouds. The roads were empty, and vast plains stretched endlessly. Occasionally, small houses appeared, but for long stretches, we seemed to be the only people surrounded by nature.

As we approached Italy, the landscape became rocky. Trees grew among large boulders, creating a different kind of beauty.

We visited a village known for its red mountains. After seeing Turkey, red mountains were not entirely new to me, but here they were different. In Turkey, the land felt lifeless. In France, the red soil was alive—with trees, animals, and even insects.

We hiked along the trails, stopping often to take photos. The contrast of green trees against bright red soil made everything look almost unreal.

We also visited a village built into the side of a mountain. Having seen Santorini, this was not entirely surprising, but it still stood out. Unlike Santorini, which is surrounded by water, this village extended endlessly across land.

From the viewpoint, sunlight painted the buildings in warm tones. Shadows cast by structures created patterns across the landscape. Inside the village, many houses were made of stone. Few people actually lived there anymore—it existed mostly for tourists.

I imagined the town without them.
